The listening habit that fills the gaps

The minutes you already spend moving are the easiest place to let scripture back in.

There is a particular kind of emptiness that arrives in the in-between moments — the twelve minutes on the train before work, the thirty minutes on the treadmill, the drive home when the day has been too much. The phone comes out almost by reflex. You are not really looking for anything. You are just filling the gap. And the gap fills itself with noise that leaves you, somehow, emptier than before.

It does not take a dramatic resolution to change this. It takes a small replacement, repeated enough times that it becomes the default.

What your body is already doing for you

Movement and repetition are old friends of memory. Monks knew this. So did the psalmists, who sang the same songs on pilgrimage year after year until the words were as familiar as their own breathing. When your hands are occupied — holding a steering wheel, gripping a dumbbell, folding laundry — your mind is actually more receptive than when you are sitting still trying to concentrate. The words have room to land.

This is why the commute and the workout are not interruptions to your devotional life. They are, potentially, the best part of it. You are already moving. You are already half-distracted from your anxieties. All you have to do is decide what goes in your ears.

The practical step is simple: before you leave the house, or before you hit play on whatever you were going to put on anyway, cue up a chapter. Not a highlight verse. A whole chapter, in a translation that sounds alive to you. Let it run. You do not have to follow along. You do not have to take notes. The goal at first is just presence — to hear the text the way you might hear a voice reading aloud in the next room.

Building the replacement, not fighting the habit

Habit research is consistent on one point that most of us already know from experience: you do not break a habit by willpower alone. You replace it. Doomscrolling is not really about the content. It is about the gesture — reach, tap, receive. The antidote is a competing gesture that is just as frictionless. If your scripture app is already open when you unlock your phone, the barrier drops to almost nothing.

Some people find it helps to anchor the new habit to an existing one. The seatbelt clicks and the chapter starts. The first mile of the run is always a psalm. Wednesday evenings, when you cook, you work through Proverbs. The specific anchor matters less than having one. Without it, the decision has to be remade every day, and the phone wins on effort alone.

Genre matters more than you might expect during movement. A lo-fi reading is perfect for a slow morning walk. Gospel or Afrobeat can carry you through a harder run in ways that a quiet narrator voice simply cannot. If you have been listening to the same passage in the same style and it has stopped landing, try it in a different room — same words, different sound, and something shifts. What accumulates over time

The most honest thing to say about this habit is that the results are slow and then suddenly obvious. For weeks it may feel like nothing is sticking. Then one afternoon, mid-commute, a line surfaces from memory at exactly the moment you need it. Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil. You did not study it. You heard it on a Tuesday in October while you were stuck in traffic. It stayed anyway.

That is what the in-between moments are for, if you let them be. Not more noise. Something that lasts.
